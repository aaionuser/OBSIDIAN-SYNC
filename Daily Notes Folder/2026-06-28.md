### Discussion

Based on the experimental data, both parts of the discovery learning lab successfully demonstrated the fundamental principles of alternating current (AC) and electromagnetic induction (EM). In Part I, the sinusoidal output and fluctuating polarity of an AC power source were confirmed by observing the voltage and current graphs side by side across different frequencies. The filament bulb reacted differently depending on these frequency shifts. At a low frequency of 0.15\text{ Hz}, the light flashed rapidly with a highly visible flicker because the alternating current reversed slowly enough for the filament to partially cool. As the frequency increased to 1.5\text{ Hz}, the bulb flickered much faster, until reaching 10\text{ Hz} where it exhibited a steady glow and emitted continuous light. This occurs because the polarity changes too rapidly for the human eye to perceive the flashing or for the filament to cool down.

In Part II, dropping a bar magnet through the center of a coil produced a distinct two-peak sinusoidal voltage profile over time, which directly demonstrates Faraday's Law of Induction. The incoming magnetic flux, measured as the area under the first curve, was found to be approximately equal to the outgoing magnetic flux measured under the second curve. Specifically, the South End trial showed an incoming flux of 0.045\text{ V}\cdot\text{s} and an outgoing flux of 0.0467\text{ V}\cdot\text{s}, while the North End yielded 0.044\text{ V}\cdot\text{s} incoming and 0.0464\text{ V}\cdot\text{s} outgoing. This close agreement between the entry and exit areas confirms that the total change in magnetic flux remains conserved throughout the induction process.

However, a noticeable difference was observed in the peak heights, where the outgoing voltage peak was consistently higher than the incoming peak. For instance, the North End produced an initial incoming peak of 0.847\text{ V} and a sharper outgoing peak of -1.482\text{ V}. This discrepancy occurs because the magnet accelerates due to gravity as it falls through the coil, resulting in a higher exit velocity. This increased speed minimizes the time interval (\Delta t), which directly maximizes the rate of change of flux (\Delta\Phi/\Delta t) and generates a higher induced electromotive force (EMF) at the exit. Additionally, the two peaks point in opposite directions because of Lenz's Law, which states that the induced current must flow in a direction that opposes the changing magnetic flux. When the magnet passes exactly through the center of the coil, the induced EMF drops to zero because the increasing flux from the leading pole is perfectly balanced out by the decreasing flux from the trailing pole, causing the net rate of change of flux to hit zero. Changing the orientation from South-first to North-first did not significantly alter the absolute peak values because the magnetic field strength is identical on both poles, though it did flip the initial direction of the induced voltage.

In terms of reliability, the data collected by the PASCO 850 Universal Interface is highly dependable, as the integration of the flux areas remained stable with a variance of less than 3\% across both runs. Keeping the voltage amplitude strictly constant at 3.0\text{ V} in Part I ensured that changes in the bulb's behavior were exclusively caused by the independent variable, which was the frequency. Minor discrepancies in the exact maximum voltage readings in Part II were primarily caused by human error during the manual drop, where releasing the magnet by hand from an approximate height of 5\text{ cm} introduces minor variations in the initial drop angle and entry speed.

To improve the experimental setup and minimize these errors in the future, a clear plastic vertical guide tube paired with a mechanical release switch should be used to standardize the dropping path and maintain a precise 5\text{ cm} height. Furthermore, connecting a high-sensitivity light sensor to an open channel would provide a quantitative wave profile of the flicker intensity to back up the manual visual observations recorded in Part I.

The phenomenon where compounds possess the same chemical formula but differ in their structural frameworks is known as isomerism. Isomers are broadly categorized into two types: constitutional isomers and stereoisomers. Constitutional isomers share the same molecular formula but possess different bonding connectivities (sequences of atom attachment). Conversely, stereoisomers possess the same molecular formula and identical atom connectivity, yet they differ strictly in the three-dimensional spatial arrangement (configuration) of their atoms. Even minor configurational variations between a pair of stereoisomers can result in entirely distinct physical and chemical properties.

​Understanding the concept of chirality is essential for identifying stereogenic centers. Stereoisomers are subdivided into enantiomers and diastereomers. Diastereomers are stereoisomers that are not mirror images of one another, whereas enantiomers are non-superimposable mirror images. Enantiomers typically occur when a molecule contains a stereogenic center, which is defined as a tetrahedral, sp^3-hybridized carbon atom bonded to four distinct atoms or functional groups. However, the presence of stereogenic centers does not inherently render a molecule chiral; compounds possessing two or more stereogenic centers may be chiral or achiral. By definition, chiral molecules are non-superimposable on their mirror images, while achiral molecules are superimposable on their mirror images and possess an internal plane of symmetry.

​To experimentally evaluate these structural theories, molecular ball-and-stick models are utilized. 

The experimental procedure dictates that stereogenic centers and molecular chirality are identified first. Subsequently, the Cahn-Ingold-Prelog (CIP) priority rules are applied to determine the absolute configuration (R/S descriptors) of the enantiomeric pairs. Finally, the exact stereoisomeric relationships between compounds containing two stereogenic centers are systematically established, distinguishing between enantiomers, diastereomers, and meso compounds.
